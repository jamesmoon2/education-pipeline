Module 2 · Feedback, delay, and accumulation

A result can become its own cause.

Feedback is what makes a system behave over time. The difficult part is that the loop is often longer—and the evidence later—than the decision-maker can see from one incident.

Read the loop, not just the arrow

A causal arrow says that one thing influences another. A loop says that the influence eventually returns to alter its starting condition. That return path changes the question from “What caused this result?” to “What behavior does this structure keep producing?”

Reinforcing loops amplify movement: adoption creates advocacy, advocacy creates more adoption. Balancing loops resist movement: a service backlog triggers added capacity, which reduces the backlog. Neither is inherently good or bad. Their value depends on the outcome, the delay, and the boundary you chose.

Cross-training: transfer your budgeting judgment

What transfers

A budget variance is a signal produced by a process over time. Your instinct to ask when a cost was committed, recognized, and reported is the same instinct needed to find a delay in a feedback loop.

Where it breaks

A causal map is not a ledger. Its arrows describe influence, not accounting identity. Do not treat an unlabeled loop as proof that the quantities balance.

Why delay changes the decision

Suppose a manager adds capacity every week until the backlog falls. If staffing changes take three weeks to affect resolved cases, the manager will make the same correction three times before seeing the result of the first. When all three changes arrive, the team has excess capacity and reverses course. The system oscillates even though each local decision looked reasonable.

When “find the loop” misleads

People can draw a loop around almost any story. A useful loop names observable variables, marks the direction of influence, identifies a return path, and predicts a pattern you could disconfirm.

Tripwire: if changing the diagram would not change any decision or prediction, it is probably narrative decoration.

Check your diagnosis

A team repeatedly raises its weekly service target. Performance improves briefly, then misses by an even larger amount. Which clue should you investigate first?
